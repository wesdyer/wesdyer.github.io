# Real-World Wind for Small-Boat Racing: A Research Report for Regatta's Wind Fields

Prepared 1 Nov 2026. Scope: what real wind does at the scale of a 5–10 minute, 1–3 km dinghy race, with numbers where they exist, and how those phenomena could become game mechanics. Every substantive claim carries a source URL. Where sources conflict, the conflict is called out (see also §9).

A note on source quality. The best quantitative sources here are meteorological: the WMO/Harper gust-factor table, Stull's *Practical Meteorology*, the Navy gap-wind primer, and the Met Office boundary-layer critiques on Frank Singleton's site. Sailing-coaching sources (Sailing World, Speed & Smarts, World Sailing coach courses, Practical Sailor) provide the tactical rules of thumb. Bethwaite's own books are not online, so his numbers come through secondary summaries (SailZing, forum quotes) and are flagged as such.

## 1. Wind fundamentals for sailors

### 1.1 True, apparent, and "tide" wind
- **Apparent wind** is the vector sum of true wind and the boat's own motion. **Current adds a third vector.** A boat drifting in current feels a "tide wind" equal and opposite to the current: "If you drift downstream at four knots, you will feel what appears to be a four-knot wind" ([World Sailing RM manual via SSCBC](https://sscbc.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Apparemt-Wind-and-Current-WS-RM-Manual-1.pdf)).
- World Sailing's coach course: current *against* the wind raises the wind a boat feels; current *with* the wind lowers it. A current running at an angle to the wind lifts one tack and heads the other ([World Sailing TCC L3 Tides & Currents](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22155807/Tide-and-Current.pdf)).
- Worked example with a 1 kt current, 6 kt wind and a slow model yacht: beating against the current, the apparent wind sits about 40° off the bow (point ~10° higher); beating with it, about 26° (foot ~5° lower). The boat went from correctly powered to overpowered just by sailing into the current zone ([onemetre.net, Current & tide](https://onemetre.net/Race/Current-tide/Current.htm)).
- **Wind against tide** makes short, steep, standing waves that slow boats ([PredictWind glossary](https://predictwind.com/glossary/w/wind-against-current); [SSCBC/World Sailing](https://sscbc.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Apparemt-Wind-and-Current-WS-RM-Manual-1.pdf)).

### 1.2 Gradient vs surface wind, and the boundary layer
- The gradient (geostrophic) wind blows roughly along the isobars 1000–2000 m up and is "significantly stronger than the surface wind" ([World Sailing TCC L3 Wind Strategy](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)).
- **Friction slows and backs (turns anticlockwise, in the NH) the surface wind.** World Sailing's coach course: over the sea, speed is reduced about **30%** and direction backs about **15°**. Over land, speed is reduced about **50%** and direction backs **40–50°** ([World Sailing TCC L3](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)). Other sources give a cross-isobar angle of **10–25° over water** and **30–45° over land** ([WRAL archive](https://www.wral.com/archive/2337903/)), with 45° as a theoretical ceiling ([AMS Glossary, Ekman spiral](https://glossarytest.ametsoc.net/wiki/Ekman_spiral)). The reason is roughness: a few mm over sea against a few cm over land ([EUMeTrain](https://resources.eumetrain.org/satmanu/CMs/CoConv/navmenu.php?page=2.0.0)).
- **Wind at deck height vs mast top.** Stull's log-profile examples: neutral air gives 5.9 m/s at 50 m and 6.4 m/s at 100 m. A stable profile with the same surface stress gives 11.3 and 17.3 m/s, almost linear with height ([Stull, Practical Meteorology §18](https://geo.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Meteorology_and_Climate_Science/Practical_Meteorology_(Stull)/18%3A_Atmospheric_Boundary_Layer/18.03%3A_Section_4-)). For sailors, masthead wind can be up to **double** deck-level wind and differ in direction by up to **30°**. Bethwaite measured large gradients in stable air even in 10 kt, and none in unstable air down to 3–5 kt ([SailZing summarising Bethwaite](https://sailzing.com/wind-shear-and-gradient/)).

### 1.3 Diurnal cycle
- **Over land, daytime heating deepens the mixed layer** (about 300 m at 9 AM, filling the boundary layer by 3 PM in Stull's example). Fast air aloft is mixed down, so surface winds are strongest and gustiest in the afternoon. At night a stable layer decouples the surface from the flow above: near-surface wind is lightest, and a nocturnal jet forms a few hundred metres up ([Stull §18](https://geo.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Meteorology_and_Climate_Science/Practical_Meteorology_(Stull)/18%3A_Atmospheric_Boundary_Layer/18.03%3A_Section_4-); [NWS training doc](https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/front/14feb-front.pdf)).
- **Over the open ocean the cycle is weak or reversed.** Sea-surface temperature barely changes day to night, and one study finds ocean winds also peaking at night ([Ashkenazy & Yizhaq, arXiv 2207.01103](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.01103)).

### 1.4 Stability: the master switch for gustiness
- **Unstable air** (cold air over warm water or land, cumulus, showers) mixes fast air down in irregular bursts. Gusts are strong and irregular, and the gradient near the surface vanishes ([boatdesign.net quoting Kenn Batt](https://www.boatdesign.net/goto/post?id=755551); [Singleton/Brettle, Gusts](http://weather.mailasail.com/franks-weather/gusts-and-other-wind-changes)).
- **Stable air** (warm air over cold water, under a high, the warm sector of a low, at night) gives light surface winds, weak and random gusts, and strong shear with height. The surface can be "glassy" while the masthead has breeze ([Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/franks-weather/gusts-and-other-wind-changes); [SailZing](https://sailzing.com/wind-shear-and-gradient/)).
- **Haze is a stability sign.** "When the atmosphere is hazy the air is stable... the gradient wind cannot come down" ([World Sailing TCC L3](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)).
- **Behind a cold front**, winds sit closer to the isobars and are "much more gusty than ahead of the front." For the same isobar spacing they are also stronger in post-frontal NW air than in a warm-sector SW ([Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/franks-weather/gusts-and-other-wind-changes)).

## 2. Wind shifts

### 2.1 Oscillating shifts
- **Typical high-pressure day:** direction oscillates **15–20° every 5–20 minutes** ([Practical Sailor](https://www.practical-sailor.com/mailport-ps-advisor/playing-the-wind-shifts-for-fun-or-sport/)).
- **Rolls** (Bethwaite and Stuart Walker, via SailZing): more likely above ~8 kt. Periods run **as short as 3 min in heavy air** and **10+ min in light-medium air**. Range is about **±5° in light-medium air**, up to **40° in heavier air** ([SailZing, Spectrum of the Wind](https://sailzing.com/spectrum-of-the-wind/)).
- **Trade winds** wander about **±15°** irregularly, with direction changes inside about half an hour ([Practical Sailor offshore log](https://www.practical-sailor.com/issues/28_3/features/4640-1.html)). In the southern Caribbean, direction is steady about 90% of the time ([Dockwalk](https://www.dockwalk.com/news/caribbean-cruising-weather-what-s-in-store)).
- **When shifts are too fast to play:** if the period is under ~2 minutes, tacking losses can exceed the gains ([Raymarine Race Master guide](https://www.raymarine.com/en-gb/learning/online-guides/raymarine-wireless-race-master-guide)).
- **Short beat vs period:** if shifts come every 5–8 minutes, a short beat may see only one shift. The play is then to head toward where the next shift will come from, not to tack on every header ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/?p=71735)).
- **Theoretical payoff:** hitting oscillations correctly can improve distance made good by **20–30%** "in theory" ([Practical Sailor](https://www.practical-sailor.com/mailport-ps-advisor/playing-the-wind-shifts-for-fun-or-sport/)).

### 2.2 Persistent and geographic shifts
- A **persistent shift** keeps going one way through the leg, for example a building sea breeze veering about 10°/h. The rule is to sail toward it ("sail toward the next shift"), which is the same as digging into the favoured side. You end up on a higher ladder rung than boats that went the other way ([Dellenbaugh, Speed & Smarts via US Sailing](https://www.ussailing.org/news/speed-smarts-sail-toward-the-next-shift/)).
- Don't over-commit. If you expect only 5° right, don't give up more than 5° of line bias to get there ([Dellenbaugh, Windcheck](https://www.windcheckmagazine.com/article/sail-fast-up-the-first-beat-in-persistent-shifts)). Don't reach the layline early, because a continuing shift makes you overstand ([Sailing World, Windshift Rules of Thumb](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/windshift-rules-of-thumb/)).
- **Geographic shifts** near land grow as you approach the shore, so they are treated as persistent ([Windcheck via search summary](https://windcheckmagazine.com/article/look-for-changes-in-wind-direction)). Lakes show oscillating, persistent and geographic shifts at once, and "oscillations that slow down effectively become persistent shifts" ([US Sailing Lake Sailing Tips](https://www.ussailing.org/news/lake-sailing-tips)).
- A **long-period oscillation is indistinguishable from a persistent shift** within one beat. Racers have to judge early which phase the breeze is in ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/?p=71735)).

### 2.3 Lifts, headers, ladder rungs, laylines
- **Ladder rungs** are lines perpendicular to the wind. A shift tilts every rung at once, so **gain = f(shift angle, lateral separation)**. Rule of thumb: **a Z° shift gains about 2Z% of lateral separation**, so 5° gives 10% and 10° gives 20%. Wider tacking angles give slightly more. Example: two boats 20 lengths apart, tacking through 70°, a 10° right shift gives the right boat about 4 lengths ([Speed & Smarts Ladder Rungs pt 2](https://canadianboating.ca/speed-smarts/speed-smarts-ladder-rungs-part-2-wind-shifts/)). With a 90° tacking angle the figures are 12% for 5°, 25% for 10° and 37% for 15°; 1° is worth about 2.25% ([search summary of Sailing World/Speed & Smarts tables](https://www.sailingworld.com/racing/why-wind-shifts-matter-more/)).
- **Header vs lift is doubled in effect:** a 5° lift is like pointing at 40° instead of 45°, and the alternative to tacking is sailing the 5° header ([Sailing World, Why Wind Shifts Matter More](https://www.sailingworld.com/racing/why-wind-shifts-matter-more/)).
- **Tack on headers past the median.** Example: starboard headings range 240–270°, median 255°. At 245° you tack; at 260° you stay. Stay off laylines in oscillating wind. Within one oscillation period of the mark, treat the oscillation as persistent ([Sailing World Rules of Thumb](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/windshift-rules-of-thumb/)). Tack once headed by more than ~5° ([Practical Sailor](https://www.practical-sailor.com/mailport-ps-advisor/playing-the-wind-shifts-for-fun-or-sport/)).
- **Line bias:** the middle of the line is a fine home base on a shifty lake until one end is favoured by more than about 15° ([US Sailing](https://www.ussailing.org/news/lake-sailing-tips)).

### 2.4 Play the shifts or play a side?
- **Wind strength decides.** In light air, pressure dominates; in heavy air (at hull speed), shifts dominate. A 5° shift is offset by about a 3 kt puff in medium air, but in light air a small puff beats it. A 15° shift at 7 kt is not offset by any puff ([Sailing World, Why Wind Shifts Matter More](https://www.sailingworld.com/racing/why-wind-shifts-matter-more/)).
- Dellenbaugh's subjective weights, pressure vs shifts: light upwind **70/30**, light downwind **80/20**, heavy upwind **20/80**, heavy downwind **40/60**. "The lighter the wind, the more you should lean toward finding more pressure, no matter what leg" ([Speed & Smarts, Light/Heavy Air pt 2](https://canadianboating.ca/speed-smarts/speed-smarts-light-and-heavy-air-differences-part-2-the-effect-of-wind-velocity-on-strategic-choices/)).

## 3. Gusts and lulls

### 3.1 Physical causes
- **Convective mixing:** in unstable air, faster air from aloft is brought down in bursts. Shower downdrafts are a separate, more violent mechanism: rain drags air down "as fast as the rain falls" and it spreads radially. Plain **mechanical turbulence** from surface friction and shear makes small, random gusts ([Singleton/Brettle, Gusts](http://weather.mailasail.com/franks-weather/gusts-and-other-wind-changes)).
- **Organised convection:** the 1928–29 Cardington study found eddies of about **1000 × 200 m** in unstable air, moving with the wind. Wind "would suddenly increase and then decrease slowly." On some days there were also 20+ km eddies, and strong gusts usually veered. Under an inversion, gusts were smaller and random in direction ([Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/franks-weather/gusts-and-other-wind-changes)).
- **Roll vortices and cloud streets:** horizontal rolls aligned within a few degrees of the wind, spaced about **2–3× the boundary-layer depth (order 1 km)**, sometimes 5 km. They organise gusts into lanes ([Stull, Cloud Organization](https://geo.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Meteorology_and_Climate_Science/Practical_Meteorology_(Stull)/06%3A_Clouds/6.03%3A_Cloud_Organization); [EUMETSAT/CIRA](https://rammb.cira.colostate.edu/wmovl/vrl/tutorials/satmanu-eumetsat/satmanu/CMs/ClStr/index.htm)).

### 3.2 Gust factors: how much stronger is a gust?
WMO-commissioned draft conversion factors for the peak 3-second gust against the 10-minute mean, at 10 m ([Harper, Kepert & Ginger 2008, AMS](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/138064.pdf)):

| Exposure | 3 s gust / 10 min mean | 60 s gust / 10 min mean |
|---|---|---|
| At sea (open water) | **1.23** | 1.05 |
| Off-sea (onshore wind at the coast) | **1.38** | 1.11 |
| Off-land (offshore wind at the coast) | **1.52** | 1.16 |
| In-land (open terrain) | **1.66** | 1.21 |

This is the key quantitative backing for "wind blowing off land is gustier." The same mean wind gusts about 25% harder (1.52 vs 1.23) just downwind of a shore than far out at sea.

- **Sailing-level figures:** about **+15%** gust factor is common up to ~12 kt on smooth water, rising with wind and waves, to about +40% above gale force ([Kenn Batt via boatdesign.net](https://www.boatdesign.net/goto/post?id=755551)). UK forecasts warn gusts "may be 40 per cent, or higher, than the average" ([Chris Tibbs, Yachting World](https://www.yachtingworld.com/features/chris-tibbs-weather-briefing-gusts-and-squalls-70268)). SailZing gives +30–40% in a gust ([SailZing](https://sailzing.com/spectrum-of-the-wind/)).
- Gust speed deltas near clouds: **2–5 kt** near patchy cumulus, **5–10 kt** near light-to-moderate rain clouds ([Practical Sailor](https://www.practical-sailor.com/mailport-ps-advisor/playing-the-wind-shifts-for-fun-or-sport/)).

### 3.3 Gust size, spacing, speed of travel, lifetime
These figures are partly Bethwaite-via-secondary; the attribution is noted in each item.
- **Fast flutter:** ±7% every 6–12 s, present even in "smooth" lake breezes and not tied to visible gusts (Bethwaite via [SailZing](https://sailzing.com/spectrum-of-the-wind/)).
- **Gust/lull cycle:** about **every 60 s, regardless of wind speed**. At any moment the water is about **50% gust, 50% lull**. A gust **persists 2–4 min** on the water before decaying to the gradient wind (Bethwaite via [SailZing](https://sailzing.com/spectrum-of-the-wind/)). In very light air a boat may see about **4 changes per 60 s** (Bethwaite via [SailZing light air](https://sailzing.com/light-air-boat-speed/)).
- **Gust size:** **200–300 m crosswind in 10–15 kt** (Bethwaite via forum paraphrase, [boatdesign.net](https://www.boatdesign.net/goto/post?id=755551)). SailZing, in its own voice, gives **100–200 m in 10–12 kt, about double in 20 kt** ([SailZing](https://sailzing.com/spectrum-of-the-wind/)). Cardington's convective eddies measured about 1000 × 200 m ([Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/franks-weather/gusts-and-other-wind-changes)).
- **Surges** (bigger, softer pressure areas): **+10–20%**, **1–5 km across** on Sydney Harbour (suggested **0.5–2 km** for smaller water), lasting **2–12 min**, drifting downwind at about **¼ of the wind speed**, and they "may disappear before you reach them" (Bethwaite via [SailZing](https://sailzing.com/spectrum-of-the-wind/)).
- **Gust/lull durations** of "a second or two through to a minute" ([Batt via boatdesign.net](https://www.boatdesign.net/goto/post?id=755551)). Coaching examples: a lifting puff of 20 s, small directional puffs of 7–10 s, a 15 s lull then 20 s of building breeze ([Sailing World, How to Call Puffs](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/how-to-call-puffs-upwind-and-down/)).
- **Squalls vs gusts:** a squall lasts minutes, a gust seconds ([Wikipedia: Squall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squall)). The official definition is a rise of at least 16 kt, to over 22 kt, lasting at least 1 min ([Tibbs](https://www.yachtingworld.com/features/chris-tibbs-weather-briefing-gusts-and-squalls-70268)). Squalls run 1–10 min ([Batt](https://www.boatdesign.net/goto/post?id=755551)).
- **Speed of travel:** the classic picture is that gusts move with the wind; Cardington's eddies "generally moved with the wind" ([Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/franks-weather/gusts-and-other-wind-changes)). Bethwaite's larger surges drift at only about ¼ wind speed. Coaching sources give no ratio, but count down a puff's arrival by watching how fast the dark patch approaches; faster-moving puffs are usually stronger ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/how-to-call-puffs-upwind-and-down/)).
- **Upwind vs downwind exposure (World Sailing):** a 300 m pressure patch is crossed in about **39 s** upwind (10 kt wind + 5 kt boat = 7.7 m/s closing) but takes about **117 s** downwind (10 − 5 = 2.6 m/s). Downwind, shifts and pressure therefore act about three times longer per patch ([World Sailing TCC L3 Downwind Tactics](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22135536/Downwind-Tactics.pdf)).

### 3.4 How gusts look and spread
- **Dark patches:** capillary ripples raised by the gust darken the water ([AMS Glossary, cat's paw](https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/cat-s-paw/)). A puff "start[s] as a small patch, then spread[s] outward in a half-moon shape downwind" ([Sailing World search summary](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/how-to-call-puffs-upwind-and-down/)). A wind shadow shows as smooth, silky water ([Windcheck via search](https://windcheckmagazine.com/article/dont-lurk-in-wind-shadows)).
- **Fan puffs** (cat's paws or "helicopter puffs") are air dropping from aloft that spreads radially from its impact point. Boats right of centre get a left shift and boats left of centre a right shift (looking downwind with the puff). On a beat, the edges of a fan are lifts on both tacks ([SpinSheet, What's the Shape of the Wind](https://www.spinsheet.com/racing/whats-shape-wind); [US Sailing](https://www.ussailing.org/news/lake-sailing-tips)). The fan phase gives way to a **directional puff** that moves straight on; "a puff that is still widening sideways is still fanning" ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/how-to-call-puffs-upwind-and-down/)).
- **Line puffs** have a straight or broadly arced leading edge and shift the wind toward the side they come from. A line from top-right brings a right shift, fairly consistently ([SpinSheet](https://www.spinsheet.com/racing/whats-shape-wind)). Wind lines are usually geographic (the sea breeze is the main example) and fill toward the back of the fleet first ([Snipe, Reading the Shape of the Wind](https://www.snipe.org/articles-advices-and-education/technical-experts/tactics-strategy/reading-the-shape-of-the-wind/)).
- **Helicopter puffs** "won't travel very far down the course." The best pressure and the lift are on their edges. If puffs last over a minute, use each one to reach the next ([Snipe](https://www.snipe.org/articles-advices-and-education/technical-experts/tactics-strategy/reading-the-shape-of-the-wind/)).
- **Downwind puff entry:** entering a puff usually gives a brief header, then a lift as you reach the centre. Don't chase puffs abeam, because they will be gone. Puff interiors are more turbulent, while edges are crisper but weaker ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/how-to-call-puffs-upwind-and-down/)).

### 3.5 Do gusts veer? (Contested; see §9)
- **Folk rule:** gusts bring down veered (NH) air from aloft, so gusts veer and lulls back. World Sailing's coach course teaches that under a cloud you get a lull and a backed wind, and between clouds a gust and a veer ([World Sailing TCC L3](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)). This rule comes from a Met Office study of 1928–29 and was popularised by Watts and Houghton.
- **Counter-evidence:** Bethwaite's 1976 Montreal Olympic data showed no veering. Met Office 1980s studies found that, of 132 gusts, "as many backed as veered." "No peer-reviewed papers ... giving any evidence for 'veering in gusts'" exist ([Singleton/Brettle](http://weather.mailasail.com/franks-weather/gusts-and-other-wind-changes)); Brettle's later study clustered gust angles around a mean of zero ([Dagley, Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/mysteries-of-the-shoreline-wind/)).
- **Middle ground:** veering is most likely in unstable convective air. Shower gusts can turn **30–40° right** (stronger with more unstable air and taller clouds), then turn back ([yacht.de summary via search](https://www.yacht.de/en/meteorology/weather-knowledge-strong-gusts-out-of-the-blue-development-and-sail-handling)).

## 4. Geography and local effects

### 4.1 Wind shadows behind obstacles
| Source | Shadow / disturbance length |
|---|---|
| World Sailing coach course, ship | shadow about **10× height**; wind "fully undisturbed" at **30× height** ([TCC L3](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)) |
| World Sailing, woods on the shore | wind clear at **30× tree height** ([TCC L3](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)) |
| World Sailing, upwind of a hill | wind starts lifting **5× height** before the obstruction, so there is less wind right next to it ([TCC L3](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)) |
| SurferToday (generic) | sheltered shadow **7–15× height** ([summary](https://www.surfertoday.com/windsurfing/what-is-a-wind-shadow)) |
| Halifax planning study, buildings | wake **8–30× height**; quiet zone about **8×** ([Halifax](https://www.halifax.ca/sites/default/files/documents/business/planning-development/applications/20218_Wind_and_Shadow_Analysis.pdf)) |
| Kenn Batt | gustier air up to **~30×** obstacle height downwind of cliffs or islands ([boatdesign.net](https://www.boatdesign.net/goto/post?id=755551)) |
| Strathclyde | porous trees give **weaker, shorter** shelter; turbulence zone about **3× height** ([ESRU](https://www.esru.strath.ac.uk/EandE/Web_sites/05-06/wind_resource/wind/wind03.html)) |
| Boat-to-boat | shadow about **4–5 mast heights** (forum), up to about **10× in light air**. In 7 kt you may see 5 kt in a shadow ([Windcheck](https://windcheckmagazine.com/article/dont-lurk-in-wind-shadows)) |

- **Shape on a river:** a single tree or building casts a **narrow** shadow that spreads with distance, so pass close to it. For large blocks, pass about **⅔ of the way across** the river to avoid both the shadow and the disturbed air reflected off the far bank ([PBO, Norfolk Broads tips](https://www.pbo.co.uk/cruising/12-tips-sailing-norfolk-broads-17423)).
- **Edge acceleration:** trees and buildings create holes, but also "sweet spots that have more wind, more often" ([US Sailing](https://www.ussailing.org/news/lake-sailing-tips)). Wind going around an obstruction accelerates and curves at the end ([World Sailing](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)).
- **Steep hill or cliff to windward of the water:** expect reverse eddies near the cliff, so avoid the area right under it, with patchy more-wind/less-wind areas beyond ([World Sailing](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)). Wind over cliffs "splash[es] down, often with considerable strength." Onshore wind on a steep cliff lifts off some way out, leaving a back eddy and calm water at the foot ([Singleton, Coastal Wind Bends](http://weather.mailasail.com/Franks-Weather/Coastal-Wind-Variatios)).

### 4.2 Shoreline effects: bending, convergence, divergence
- **Wind slanting onto a steep coast** is deflected along it and strengthens ([Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/Franks-Weather/Coastal-Wind-Variatios)).
- **Convergence and divergence along a coast (NH):** with the coast on your **right looking downwind**, winds are stronger than in the reverse case ([Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/franks-weather/gusts-and-other-wind-changes)). Sailing World's sea-breeze guide gives the same thing from the racer's view: facing upwind with the shore on your **left** gives convergence and more wind; shore on your **right** gives divergence and lighter wind ([Sailing World, Winning the Sea Breeze Game](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/winning-the-sea-breeze-game/)). World Sailing's slide shows the same pattern, with the onshore case producing "no shift" ([TCC L3](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)). The Southern Hemisphere is mirrored.
- **Offshore wind turning as it leaves land (contested):**
  - World Sailing coach course: an offshore wind "starts shifting by **30° clockwise**" as it enters the sea, so approach a shoreside mark on starboard and tack on the first left shift ([TCC L3](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)).
  - Tibbs: "When the wind blows off the land it will increase and veer." Houghton: "the wind must veer as it moves out over the water." RYA's Saltonstall: the wind "tries to leave the shore at right angles" ([quoted in Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/Franks-Weather/Coastal-Wind-Variatios)).
  - Met Office data (Francis 1970, ~3000 obs): no consistent change overall. When the **sea was cooler than the land** (738 cases), offshore wind **backed about 6°** on average. Veering mostly happened when the sea was warmer (autumn). East-coast pairs in 1987: 22 backed vs 2 veered outside Sep/Oct, and 3 backed vs 9 veered in Sep/Oct ([Singleton/Brettle](http://weather.mailasail.com/Franks-Weather/Coastal-Wind-Variatios)).
  - The "right-angle refraction" idea traces to Alan Watts (1965), who used Snell's law. Dagley found no validating source and concludes that local observation beats theory ([Dagley, Sailing World 2013](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/mysteries-of-the-shoreline-wind/)). Walker says a progressive back near shore is "rarely evident except in stable offshore flows."
- **Practical lake rule:** look for **accelerated flow in a near-shore band**, or a **header on the tack pointing at the shore**, so you can tack onto a lifted offshore tack. Shoreline friction tends to give **port-tack lifts near a left-side shore** in the NH ("dare I say usually?") ([US Sailing Lake Sailing Tips](https://www.ussailing.org/news/lake-sailing-tips)).

### 4.3 Headlands, capes, points
- **Corner or cape effect:** wind can be **5–10 kt stronger** off a headland and gustier ([boats.com UK](https://uk.boats.com/how-to/safe-sailing-avoid-hazardous-waters/)). A first-hand account describes light wind in an island's lee, then 35+ kt "as we cleared the end of it" ([Blue View](https://justalittlefurther.com/just-a-little-further/tag/cape+effect)). Wind bends round headlands and strengthens as it is squeezed ([Yachting Monthly](https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/how-to-plan-and-sail-a-passage-round-a-headland-2-33006)).
- **California coast (expansion fans and compression bulges):** with summer NW flow under the marine inversion, the marine layer slows and thickens on the upwind side of a cape (compression bulge, convergence). It accelerates and thins downwind (expansion fan, divergence), clearing cloud. Separate surface wind maxima above 10 m/s form downwind of Cape Blanco, Cape Mendocino and Point Arena, and a strong fan sits downwind of Point Conception ([Rahn et al. 2013](https://meteo.ku.edu/darahn/pubs/Rahn_etal_2013.pdf); [AMS confex](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/43682.pdf); [UCAR/EOL](https://flight.eol.ucar.edu/publications/aircraft-measurements-and-numerical-simulations-expansion-fan-california-coast)). Signatures for the game: **slower, cloudier air on the upwind side of a point; faster, clearer, divergent air past it.**
- **Hill speed-up:** flow over a smooth hill or ridge speeds up at the crest, with the fractional speed-up scaling roughly with slope H/L (Jackson & Hunt theory; Askervein field benchmark) ([Askervein benchmark](https://wemep.readthedocs.io/en/latest/windconditions/benchmarks/askervein.html); [Walmsley & Taylor](https://data-ww3.ifremer.fr/BIB/Walmsley_Taylor_BLM1996.pdf)). The commonly quoted ΔS ≈ 2H/L for 2-D ridges is not confirmed verbatim in these sources.

### 4.4 Channelling, gaps, venturi
- **Gap winds:** typically **20–60 kt**, with effects 10–100 miles downstream. They are shallow (hundreds to a few thousand feet), and the **strongest winds are at the exit**, where the flow fans out, not in the throat. Venturi effects matter mainly for gaps of a few km or less. Friction cuts frictionless Bernoulli exit speeds by **15–40%**. Gibraltar's Levanter can go from near calm in the Alboran basin to gale on the west side ([Navy Mesoscale Primer: Gap Winds](https://atmos.uw.edu/~cliff/Navygap4.html)). The Columbia Gorge peaks near its exit too ([NOAA TA](https://www.weather.gov/media/wrh/online_publications/TAs/ta9811.pdf)).
- **Canary Islands wind acceleration zones (WAZ):** approach wind 10–15 kt gains **10–20 kt** in the channels and round island ends, going "from near calm to Force 5 in minutes." The change happens within a couple of hundred metres, from 5 kt in the lee to 25+ kt in the zone ([No Frills Sailing](https://no-frills-sailing.com/wind-acceleration-zone-waz-explained/); [MailASail Griselda blog](https://blog.mailasail.com/griselda/posts/2022/10/13/15-acceleration-zones-and-lazy-lines)). Tenerife's lee shadow can stretch about **20 nm** and its position is hard to predict ([yacht.de Tenerife](https://www.yacht.de/en/travel-and-charter/worldwide/canary-islands-tenerife-in-the-shadow-of-the-giants/)).
- **Hawaii:** the Big Island and Maui split the trades, producing tip jets and channel jets, with the strongest channel wind (>9 m/s) at the exit. Wakes form in the lee ([Hitzl et al., via par.nsf.gov](https://par.nsf.gov/search/author:%22Hitzl,%20David%20Eugene%22)). Alenuihaha channel winds can exceed 35 kt ([search summary](https://moderngeographic.com/sail-hawaii)). In the Kona lee, daytime brings a **reversed westerly flow of ~4 m/s** merging with sea breezes, and the island wake is a zone of weak winds ([Yang, Chen & Fujioka 2009 via search](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/37155.pdf); [NASA, Little Islands Big Wake](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/news/feature-articles/little-islands-big-wake)).
- **Rivers and valleys:** inside a river valley "the winds tend almost always to get redirected along the direction of the river," so most sailing is running or beating ([Norton, Decoding the Winds, Pittsburgh](https://sites.pitt.edu/~jdnorton/sail/Decoding_winds/index.html)). A bend that looks like it will free you "will often just head you." Sail up the inside of bends, and hug the windward bank for the land-driven lift before the wind swings to the down-river direction ([PBO Broads](https://www.pbo.co.uk/cruising/12-tips-sailing-norfolk-broads-17423)). Side valleys inject crossflows where they meet the river, giving calms, eddies and erratic gusts at the junction ([Norton](https://sites.pitt.edu/~jdnorton/sail/Decoding_winds/index.html)).
- **Lakes in mountains:** valley channelling stops the sea or lake breeze from veering ([Yachting Monthly](https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/understand-sea-breeze-49027)). Lake Garda is the textbook case. The **Pelèr** blows N about 7–11/12 AM at 10–25 kt (often 20, sometimes 30); the **Ora** blows S from about 12–1 PM to 6 PM. The narrow north end is strongest ([360 Garda Life](https://360gardalife.com/en/lake-garda/lake-garda-winds); [Garda Trentino](https://www.gardatrentino.it/en/outdoor/water-sports/winds)).

### 4.5 Katabatic, anabatic, glacier and fjord winds
- **Glacier/katabatic magnitudes vary hugely with scale:**
  - Svalbard fjord (Kongsfjorden): katabatic flow up to about **5 m/s** near the ground. The fjord wind field is "highly variable" from the mix of along-fjord flow and local katabatics, and the Arctic boundary layer is about **250 m** deep ([Polar Research](https://polarresearch.net/index.php/polar/article/view/7884/14994)).
  - NW Greenland glaciers: up to **14 m/s** at 40–110 m altitude. SE Greenland (Ammassalik): over **20 m/s** in 1–2 day events, 4–8 per year ([search summary of Polar Research](https://polarresearch.net/index.php/polar/article/download/7884/14993?inline=1)).
  - **Piteraq** (E Greenland): 50–80 m/s typical in events, about 90 m/s worst recorded, mostly autumn and winter ([Wikipedia: Piteraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piteraq)).
  - Patagonian San Rafael glacier wind: >100 m thick, about **5 m/s** maximum, and suppressed when winds aloft are strong ([JMSJ](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jmsj1965/67/1/67_1_99/_article)).
- **Fjord channelling:** wind usually blows **along** the fjord one way or the other, either forced by the along-axis synoptic wind or pressure-driven when the synoptic wind is crosswise ([Polar Research, Suomi et al.](https://polarresearch.net/index.php/polar/article/view/9263); [Noonsite](https://www.noonsite.com/report/useful-tips-on-cruising-norway/)). Cruising sailors report "flat calms and violent gusts blowing almost vertically down the sides of the sheer cliffs" ([Noonsite](https://www.noonsite.com/report/useful-tips-on-cruising-norway/)). **Fall winds** are strongly developed on the Norwegian coast ([AMS Glossary: fall wind](https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/fall-wind/)).
- **Williwaws:** "a sudden violent gust of cold land air common along mountainous coasts of high latitudes" (Strait of Magellan, Aleutians) ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/williwaws)). FitzRoy described the sea surface struck by them as "covered with foam, which is taken up by them, and flies before their fury until dispersed in vapour," with ships at anchor thrown onto their beam ends ([U. Chile library edition](https://libros.uchile.cl/files/presses/1/monographs/22/submission/proof/94/)). They hit the water at gale force out of calm, are gone within minutes, are local, are absent from forecasts, and can come from any direction ([Nammert, Magellan/Beagle hazards](https://www.nammert.com/gefahrenstelle-magellanstrasse-und-beagle-kanal.php?lang=en)). Alaskan williwaws have reached nearly 140 mph ([UTMSI](https://utmsi.utexas.edu/science-and-the-sea/radio-program/williwaws/)).

### 4.6 Sea breeze, lake breeze, land breeze
- **Trigger:** a land–sea air temperature difference of **1–2 °C** is enough to start it ([World Sailing TCC L3](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)). Sailing World says as little as 4° (unit unstated) for a pure sea breeze ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/winning-the-sea-breeze-game/)). Cumulus forming over land or hills shows the circulation has begun; a cloud over the sea dissolving means the flow is moving shoreward ([World Sailing](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)).
- **Timeline (theoretical day):** 8–10 calm; 10–11 patchy fill near shore; 12–2 builds from shore outward; 2–4 peak; 4–5 drops quickly ([PredictWind, Sea Breeze](https://help.predictwind.com/en/articles/10449446-marine-meteorology-4-sea-breeze)). It is established by late morning ([Yachting Monthly](https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/understand-sea-breeze-49027)) and created around midday, "finalised after 3 hours" ([World Sailing](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)).
- **Strength:** typically **10–20 kt** ([PredictWind](https://help.predictwind.com/en/articles/10449446-marine-meteorology-4-sea-breeze)), often 15–20 kt and over 20 on good Solent days. It can jump from F3 to F6 within an hour when it reinforces an onshore gradient ([Yachting Monthly](https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/understand-sea-breeze-49027)). It is about **1 km deep** and **10–100 km** across ([PredictWind](https://help.predictwind.com/en/articles/10449446-marine-meteorology-4-sea-breeze)).
- **Direction:** it starts roughly perpendicular to the shore, with orientation set by the coastline averaged over 10–20 miles. It then **veers about 10°/h** (northern US) or **5°/h** (southern US), continuing until about 45° to the shore ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/winning-the-sea-breeze-game/)). World Sailing gives a final direction about **20° from the shore** after 3 h ([TCC L3](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22160202/Wind-Strategy.pdf)). It may end the day parallel to the shore. Valley channelling stops the veer ([Yachting Monthly](https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/understand-sea-breeze-49027)). Backdoor sea breezes veer in **10–15° pulses**, not steadily ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/winning-the-sea-breeze-game/)).
- **Interaction with gradient wind:** an onshore gradient reinforces it. A light offshore gradient delays it; a strong one blocks it. Where the two meet, a **fluky calm zone** forms offshore that moves during the day ([Yachting Monthly](https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/understand-sea-breeze-49027)). A strong offshore gradient leaves a "hot-air wedge" a mile or more offshore, and landfall may not come until late afternoon ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/winning-the-sea-breeze-game/)).
- **The front:** a **dark band of new ripples** moving inshore, under a sharp cumulus edge with clear sky seaward ([Yachting Monthly](https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/understand-sea-breeze-49027)). If racing starts during the fight between the two winds, **both corners often gain on the middle** ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/winning-the-sea-breeze-game/)). Wind lines fill unevenly, and boats that stay in pressure gain even when the speed difference is only 1–2 kt ([Snipe, Biscayne Bay](https://www.snipe.org/articles-advices-and-education/technical-experts/tactics-strategy/reading-the-shape-of-the-wind/)).
- **Coast shape:** concave coasts may focus the breeze and convex ones disperse it. Cold water beside very hot land gives the strongest, most reliable breezes (Perth, S Morocco). Rain over land kills the breeze ([PredictWind](https://help.predictwind.com/en/articles/10449446-marine-meteorology-4-sea-breeze)).
- **SF Bay:** the Golden Gate is the only sea-level gap in the coast range, so it funnels the summer breeze at **20–30 kt** through the Gate on most afternoons. Mornings are foggy and light, and there is 3–4 kt of tidal current ([UCAR WRF abstract](https://www2.mmm.ucar.edu/wrf/users/workshops/WS2014/abstracts/4.3.pdf); [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/news/11682511/why-san-francisco-gets-so-windy-and-foggy-in-the-summer); [sea.net](https://www.sea.net/destinations/san-francisco-bay)).
- **Land breeze (night):** weaker, about **5–10 km/h** (about 3–5 kt), because the night-time temperature contrast is smaller ([GKToday](https://www.gktoday.in/land-breeze/)). It usually reaches only 1–2 miles offshore and dies once the sun warms the land ([Yachting Monthly](https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/sailing-skills/understand-sea-breeze-49027)). Satellite studies detect a land-breeze signal much farther out in the subtropics ([Gille et al. 2005](https://pordlabs.ucsd.edu/sgille/seabreeze/grl.pdf)).
- **Tidal flats:** in the German Bight, tides change the sea breeze mainly through the **flats' heat budget as they dry and flood**. Exposed sun-warmed flats act like land; tidal currents themselves barely affect surface wind ([Meteorologische Zeitschrift 2016](https://doaj.org/article/65e57ca839e14054bd53ec859f425698)). An incoming tide that brings cold water inshore speeds sea-breeze onset ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/winning-the-sea-breeze-game/)).

### 4.7 Clouds, rain and squalls
- **"Sucking" cumulus (updraft inflow).** Small cumulus cost a few knots and shift the wind about **10°**. Large, low-based growing cumulus cause a significant speed drop and shift **20–40°**. Worked example: steady 10 kt with a 2 kt inflow gives **8 kt approaching the cloud, 10 kt overhead, 12 kt just past it**. These effects are strongest in the tropics ([PredictWind, Clouds](https://help.predictwind.com/en/articles/9833316-marine-meteorology-3-clouds)).
- **"Blowing" rain clouds (downdraft outflow).** Speed rises and the shift arrives **before the rain**, sometimes with whitecaps. A cloud passing on your right gives a right shift, and on your left a left shift. Its back side has light air, and a lull lingers after it passes ([PredictWind](https://help.predictwind.com/en/articles/9833316-marine-meteorology-3-clouds)). Practical Sailor says to sail on the windward side of rain clouds and stay to windward of cumulus ([Practical Sailor](https://www.practical-sailor.com/mailport-ps-advisor/playing-the-wind-shifts-for-fun-or-sport/)).
- **Cloud lines:** under a convergence line, speed eases to near zero and direction shifts abruptly by **90° or more**. A confluent line gives a gradual shift of under 90° ([PredictWind](https://help.predictwind.com/en/articles/9833316-marine-meteorology-3-clouds)).
- **Squalls:** gusts at the leading edge, a 90°+ shift is "not unusual," and they rarely exceed 1.5–2× the wind speed ([search summary of setsail.com](https://setsail.com/squalls/)). Squalls bring the shelf or roll cloud and the cold "tickle" before the blast ([Wikipedia: Outflow boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outflow_boundary)).
- **Under a cloud centre** there can be no wind, or a near-180° shift where air is rising ([Sail Magazine via search](https://sailmagazine.com/?p=16132)).

### 4.8 Water temperature and stability
- **Cold air over warm water** is unstable: a deeper boundary layer, fast air mixed down, gusty. **Warm air over cold water** is stable, with a shallow, smooth, sheared and lighter surface layer ([Young 1998](https://data-ww3.ifremer.fr/BIB/Young_CE1998.pdf); [Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/Franks-Weather/Coastal-Wind-Variatios)). A spring sea or lake breeze over cold water is the classic shear and gradient case ([SailZing](https://sailzing.com/wind-shear-and-gradient/)).
- **Monterey Bay** (cold upwelled water, marine layer): calm mornings, and a NW breeze building to **10–20 kt by noon** or **15–25 kt by early afternoon**, easing after sunset. June and July are often fogged in until late morning, and the fog bank rolls in with the afternoon breeze. Small-craft advisories run NW 15–25 gusting 30 ([sea.net Monterey](https://www.sea.net/blog/sailing-monterey-bay-guide); [NWS PZZ535](https://forecast.weather.gov/shmrn.php?mz=PZZ535)).

## 5. Race strategy tied to wind

1. **Default plan:** sail toward the mark, keep it simple, sail in more wind, don't over-manoeuvre (Steve Hunt's tactics seminar, [SDYC notes](https://sdyc.org/documents/20124/59960/8.+tactics_sailx.pdf)). "Until you have a good reason to do something else just aim toward the finish" ([US Sailing](https://www.ussailing.org/education/adult/certification-courses-endorsements/racing-tips-resources/)).
2. **Persistent shift:** sail toward it and into the header. Don't tack on headers; take sterns ([Sailing World Rules of Thumb](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/windshift-rules-of-thumb/)).
3. **Oscillating shift:** tack on headers past the median, stay near the middle and off the laylines, and start on the lifted tack. When unsure, sail the long tack first ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/windshift-rules-of-thumb/)).
4. **Pressure vs shift:** weight by wind strength (§2.4). "Sailing 10 degrees off course increases sailing distance less than 2%" (Gladstone, North U, via search; the geometry is 1/cos 10° = 1.015). That is why a lane of 1–2 kt more wind 10° off the rhumb line almost always pays in light air. **Too much** detour to a short-lived puff loses: Ingham's example had 18 kt on the right vs 12 kt on the lift, but the 18 kt didn't last long enough ([Sailing World, Pressure or Shift](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/sailboat-racing-tips-pressure-or-shift)).
5. **Corners and risk:** "Big risk means big losses or gains." Stay with the group and cover when ahead, and stay on the side you expect more pressure from ([World Sailing Downwind Tactics](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22135536/Downwind-Tactics.pdf)). Corners pay in a sea-breeze transition ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/winning-the-sea-breeze-game/)) and in persistent shifts. They lose in oscillating wind because of overstanding.
6. **Downwind:** gybe on the lifts (the shift that lets you sail lower), stay on the headed gybe, don't gybe on a header. Sail first the inside of a wind bend, take the side you avoided upwind, and go where the current helps and the waves let you surf ([World Sailing Downwind](https://media.sailing.org/sailing/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/22135536/Downwind-Tactics.pdf); [Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/windshift-rules-of-thumb/)). Shifts and pressure act about three times longer downwind per patch (§3.3), and pressure is worth more downwind. Exception: with a persistent shift, take the first gybe away from it unless one side fills stronger ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/windshift-rules-of-thumb/)).
7. **Reaches:** bear away in puffs, head up in lulls, with 20–30° of course variation on a puffy reach. Sail a high arc early if the breeze is building or heading ([Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/windshift-rules-of-thumb/)).
8. **Meet puffs, don't chase them.** Small detours to intercept are fine. Puffs abeam are gone before you get there ([US Sailing](https://www.ussailing.org/news/lake-sailing-tips); [Sailing World](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/how-to-call-puffs-upwind-and-down/)). Dinghies should ignore downwind surges that are too far to catch (Bethwaite via [SailZing](https://sailzing.com/spectrum-of-the-wind/)).
9. **Current:** shallow water has less current, so work the side out of the adverse current. "More wind and lift is better than going through lots of current" ([SDYC/Steve Hunt](https://sdyc.org/documents/20124/59960/8.+tactics_sailx.pdf)). Wind against current makes the beat feel shorter, so tack earlier for laylines. Wind with current makes it feel longer ([ILCA Optimist Racing extract via search](https://ilca.uk/news/fernhurst-review)).

**Value of a shift in boat lengths for a 1 km beat (derived).** The ~2Z% rule gives about 20% of lateral separation for a 10° shift. Boats split 200 m (about 36 lengths of a 5.5 m boat) exchange about 40 m, or about **7 lengths**. Boats split by the full width of a 1 km beat with a 90° tacking angle (~700 m) exchange about 140 m, about **25 lengths**. This is computed from the cited rule, not quoted.

## 6. How other games and simulators model wind

- **Virtual Regatta Inshore** (the World Sailing eSailing platform; 5–10 minute races). It shows wind shifts, wind shadow, VMG, a course view with strong/weak wind indicators, and dark water for stronger wind ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_Regatta); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1182301199); [JSCA notes](https://jsca.bc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/VIRTUAL-REGATTA-NOTES-for-JERICHO-2021.pdf)). Club sailors praised the shifts, shadows and lee-bow but questioned its rules calls ([Chichester Observer](https://chichester.co.uk/sport/other-sport/dell-quay-sailors-dont-let-lockdown-stop-them-getting-their-racing-fix-2867211)).
- **Virtual Regatta Offshore / sailonline** use GRIB/GFS forecast wind (SailGrib reproduces "gfs_vr_100," updated every 6 h) ([SailGrib](https://sailgrib.com/en/apps/sailgrib-wr/aide/routing/routing-for-virtual-regattas/)). Critiques: "no land effect on wind speed or direction," a "much more 'mathematical'" feel, and a pressure hole that vanished at the next forecast update so boats sailed through what "would NOT have happened in real life" ([Melbourne–Hobart report](https://sailonline.org/static/var/sphene/sphwiki/attachment/2023/01/04/MelbourneHobart_23_REP_Archon.pdf)).
- **MarineVerse** (VR sailing) player feedback: "In real life, wind shifts are not predictable and wind speeds vary over a course." With predictable wind, a better start can't lose after the first mark. Players asked for **more, smaller, less predictable shifts**, **different speeds on different parts of the course** (e.g. 7 kt top, 15 kt bottom, gusts to 20), shifts beyond ±30°, and bigger boat wind shadows ([MarineVerse feedback](https://www.marineverse.com/feedback/marineverse-sailing-club/wind-shifts-wind-speed)).
- **SailX** (browser multiplayer): everyone is the same speed with perfect crew, so tactics dominate. "Bad air extends less" than in real life. Current is a big factor (shallows have less), and oscillations are played toward a side ([Steve Hunt seminar, SDYC](https://sdyc.org/documents/20124/59960/8.+tactics_sailx.pdf)).
- **Sailaway:** weather comes from NOAA data; waves are simulated from the wind history, current, depth and islands. Early players complained the wind was too constant ([BoatUS](https://boatus.com/expert-advice/expert-advice-archive/2017/july/cool-virtual-sailing-simulator); [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/552920/discussions/0/1318836262646567532/?ctp=5)).
- **Velaris** (indie): wind from a Perlin-noise grid that drifts over time. Stated goals are "organic, learnable, and still dynamic" ([itch.io](https://paper-crow.itch.io/velaris)). **Sailing Master** (old Mac) was praised as "a good way to learn the wind-shifts," with intensity and direction varying across the course ([Macintosh Repository](https://www.macintoshrepository.org/19652-sailing-master)). **SAILSHIFT** is a two-boat trainer about tacking at the right moment on shifts ([itch.io](https://xgl100.itch.io/sailshift)).
- **Academic models:** wind direction as a **Markov chain** for stochastic routing and risk attitude in match racing ([Tagliaferri, Philpott, Viola & Flay 2014, Ocean Engineering; Newcastle eprint](https://eprints.ncl.ac.uk/214060); Philpott & Mason 2001, cited in the [hybrid control paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.08103)). "Nested Markov chains" have been used for wind; neural nets and SVMs for short-term direction forecasting ([Tagliaferri thesis, Edinburgh](https://era.ed.ac.uk/handle/1842/16237)). Stochastic optimal control of a sailboat ([arXiv 2109.08260](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.08260)).
- **What players criticise:** wind that is too predictable or uniform (no comeback possible), no land effects, forecast discontinuities that feel unfair, and wind shadows too small. **What they praise:** readable pressure on the water, shifts that teach real tactics, and wind that is "learnable."

## 8. Design-relevant numbers

**Shifts**
- Oscillation amplitude: **±5°** (light-medium) up to **±20°** (high-pressure day, 15–20° swings), extremes **40°** in heavy air. Trades ±15°.
- Oscillation period: **3 min** (heavy air) to **10+ min** (light-medium); typical **5–20 min**. Under ~2 min, tacking isn't worth it.
- Persistent: sea-breeze veer **5–10°/h**, or pulses of **10–15°**; offshore-to-water bend **0–30°** (contested, can be ±).
- Value: **~2% of lateral separation per degree** (10° gives about 20%).

**Gusts and lulls**
- Gust factor (3 s / 10 min): **1.23 at sea, 1.38 onshore at coast, 1.52 offshore at coast, 1.66 inland**. Sailor-perceived gusts **+15%** (≤12 kt smooth water) to **+40%**. Surges **+10–20%**.
- Cloud-driven: **+2–5 kt** near cumulus, **+5–10 kt** near rain clouds; squall ≥**+16 kt**, up to **1.5–2×** mean.
- Size: gusts **100–300 m** across at 10–15 kt, about double at 20 kt; convective eddies **~1000 × 200 m**; surges **0.5–5 km**; cloud-street spacing **~1–5 km**.
- Timing: flutter ±7% every **6–12 s**; gust/lull cycle **~60 s**; individual gust life on the water **2–4 min**; surges **2–12 min**; squalls **1–10 min**.
- Travel: gusts roughly **with the wind**; Bethwaite surges about **¼ wind speed**.
- Coverage: about **50% gust / 50% lull** at any instant (unstable air).
- Gust direction: fan puffs give **opposite shifts on each side** of centre; mean veer in gusts is **contested (≈0 in Met Office data; up to 30–40° right in showers)**.

**Obstacles and shores**
- Wind shadow: **~10× height** strong shadow, **30× height** to full recovery (solid/trees); generic **7–15×**; buildings **8–30×**; porous trees weaker and shorter; upwind bulge from **5× height** ahead of an obstacle.
- Gustier air downwind of land: up to **30×** obstacle height.
- Boat shadow: **4–10 mast heights**, 7 kt → 5 kt inside it.
- Headland or cape: **+5–10 kt** locally; Canary WAZ **+10–20 kt** with transitions within **~200 m**.
- Gap wind: **20–60 kt** absolute, max at the **exit**; friction takes **15–40%** off ideal Bernoulli speed-up.
- Island lee shadow: up to **~20 nm** for a 3,700 m island.

**Thermal and diurnal**
- Sea-breeze trigger **ΔT 1–2 °C**; onset 10–11 AM, building 12–2, peak 2–4, dying 4–5 PM; strength **10–20 kt** (to 25+ with a gradient boost; SF Gate **20–30 kt**); depth **~1 km**; reach **10–100 km**.
- Land breeze **~3–5 kt**, reaching **1–2 nm** offshore (locally).
- Lake thermal (Garda): **10–25 kt**, morning N / afternoon S, switch around midday.
- Canyon reservoir afternoon gusts **45–50 mph**; storm outflow **54 mph**.

**Katabatic and fjord**
- Fjord glacier drainage **~5 m/s** (Svalbard, Patagonia) to **14 m/s** (Greenland glaciers); SE Greenland events **>20 m/s**; piteraq **50–90 m/s** (extreme).
- Williwaws: gale-force out of calm, lasting minutes, any direction; Alaska up to ~140 mph.

**Current and tide**
- Tide wind = −current vector. A 1 kt current in 6 kt wind swings apparent wind by about **10–14°** and changes the rig regime.

**Boundary layer**
- Surface backed from gradient **~10–25°** over water, **30–50°** over land; surface speed **~70%** (sea) or **~50%** (land) of gradient.
- Masthead vs deck: up to **2× speed, 30° direction** in stable air; ~0 in unstable air.

**Strategy weights**
- Pressure:shift weights: light upwind **70:30**, light downwind **80:20**, heavy upwind **20:80**, heavy downwind **40:60**.
- A **10° detour costs 1.5% distance**; a 5° shift is offset by about **3 kt** of puff in medium air.
- A pressure patch acts about **3× longer** for a downwind boat than an upwind one (300 m: 39 s vs 117 s).

## 9. Where sources disagree

1. **Do gusts veer?** Watts, Houghton and World Sailing's coach course say yes (NH). Bethwaite (1976 Olympic data) and Met Office studies (132 gusts, as many backed as veered; Brettle's mean ≈ 0) say no as a rule. Best synthesis: it may happen in organised unstable convection and showers, and is random in stable air ([Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/franks-weather/gusts-and-other-wind-changes); [Dagley](https://www.sailingworld.com/how-to/mysteries-of-the-shoreline-wind/)).
2. **Which way does offshore wind bend over water?** Tibbs, Houghton and World Sailing: it veers (World Sailing says 30°). Saltonstall/RYA: it turns perpendicular to the shore. Walker: it backs only in stable flows. Met Office data: it backs about 6° when the sea is cooler than the land, and veers mostly when the sea is warmer. There is no universal rule; it is stability-dependent ([Singleton](http://weather.mailasail.com/Franks-Weather/Coastal-Wind-Variatios)).
3. **Sea-breeze final angle:** about 45° to the shore (Sailing World), about 20° from the shore (World Sailing), "may end parallel to the shore" (Yachting Monthly). Its strength also varies (10–20 kt vs 15–25 kt).
4. **Wind-shadow length:** 4–5× (boats, forum), 7–15× (generic), 10× strong with 30× to full recovery (World Sailing), 8–30× (buildings). Porosity and stability explain much of the spread.
5. **Gust factor over sea vs land in typhoons:** one study finds off-sea gust factors about 8% higher at 10 m than over land, opposite to the WMO table ([Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2022.920048/pdf)). That is extreme-wind data; the WMO table is the better guide for normal conditions.
6. **Current and apparent wind:** World Sailing says current against the wind increases the felt wind. One Practical Sailor article says beating against the tide reduces apparent wind speed; the vector maths sides with World Sailing.
7. **Channel acceleration in Hawaii:** some studies show channel exit winds stronger than upstream; one (Hsiao et al.) found the channel exit 3–4 m/s *slower* than upstream in some regimes. It depends on regime.
8. **Gust travel speed:** "with the wind" (Cardington) vs about ¼ of wind speed for surges (Bethwaite). These likely describe different phenomena (individual gusts vs large pressure patches).

## 10. Phenomena → game mechanic ideas (brief)

- **Pressure patches as moving dark-water blobs:** 150–300 m gusts (scale with wind), about 50% coverage, a 60 s cycle, a 2–4 min life, drifting with the wind and fading at the end of life. They answer "how can I sail in more wind?" because they are visible, predictable within seconds and unreliable beyond a minute.
- **Fan puffs:** on touchdown, a radial spread that gives opposite shifts on each side of centre, then a directional phase. This rewards "enter on the lifted edge."
- **Surges:** slow (¼ wind speed), large (0.5–2 km), soft (+10–20%) patches that last 2–12 min and set the "which side" question on a leg.
- **Oscillator per venue:** amplitude and period from §8 (e.g. pond ±15° every 1–2 min; lake ±10° every 4–8 min; ocean ±5° every 10+ min). Add noise so it is learnable but not clockwork, which is MarineVerse's complaint. Keep the period at or above about 2 min, or tacking on shifts becomes pointless.
- **Persistent trend:** sea-breeze veer of 5–10°/h compressed to about 5–15° across one race, or 10–15° pulses (backdoor). Teaches "sail toward the new wind."
- **Shadows from land and props:** a strong shadow to about 10× height, recovering to 30×. Porous trees are weaker; solid cliffs give a reverse eddy at the foot. Shadows widen with distance, so pass close to a single tree.
- **Shore bands:** a near-shore acceleration band, convergence (more wind) on the shore that is on your left looking upwind (NH), and divergence on the other.
- **Points and gaps:** a cape effect of +5–10 kt with a short transition (~200 m), maximum at the exit of a gap, and a slow cloudy compression zone upwind of a point (Monterey/Otter).
- **Gust factor by fetch:** sample gust strength from the WMO table by whether the air has just left land (1.5) or crossed open water (1.2). Offshore-wind venues should feel gustier.
- **Cloud mechanics:** cumulus inflow (8 kt approaching, 12 kt behind, 10–40° shift); rain-cloud outflow (gust and shift before the rain, a lull behind); convergence lines with a 90° shift and dead air (tropical lagoon, volcano).
- **Katabatic and williwaw events:** rare, short (tens of seconds), violent (2–3× mean) spray-sheet bursts from glacier or cliff faces, from any direction, with calm patches between (fjord).
- **Current as wind:** the tide-wind vector added to apparent wind; one tack lifted when current crosses the wind; flats heating to strengthen the sea breeze at midday low water.
- **Stability knob per venue:** stable (night, cold water, swamp) gives small, random, slow gusts with glassy holes; unstable (canyon afternoon, post-front fjord) gives big, organised, fast gusts with stronger fans.
- **Readability rules (from game critiques):** make pressure visible as dark water with a short look-ahead, keep forecasts and fields continuous (no pops), give some land effects (their absence was criticised), and let wind vary across the course so a bad start can come back.
